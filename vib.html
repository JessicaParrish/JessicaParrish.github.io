    Case Study - Web Redesign

    Overview

    Role

    Product Design (UX/UI)

    Timeline

    3-4 weeks

    Tools

    Figma

    Context

    A full mobile-first redesign of a local wellness business website — fixing a broken mobile layout, sparse content structure, missing navigation, and an inaccessible footer to create a polished, conversion-ready site.

    Industry

    Wellness & Spa

    Location

    Niles, Ohio

    Project Type

    Homepage, Nav Bar, & Footer Redesign

    Final Project Links

    View Final Desktop Design View Final Mobile Design

    Problems Identified

    • Mobile Hero Collapse
      The hero image and text overlapped on mobile - the headline "Sanctuary for the Senses" and tagline were partially hidden behind the photo, making the opening completely unreadable on phones.
    • Extremely sparse homepage
      The full homepage was one photo, one paragraph, then a massive empty green void before a single-line footer. No services preview, no booking prompt, no reason to stay.
    • No call to action on the hero
      Visitors landing on the page had no button or clear next step — they had to actively hunt through the nav to find services or booking.
    • Low-contrast text on green
      Dark script font on olive/lime green sits right on the accessibility edge — especially problematic on a phone screen in sunlight.
    • Footer had no real information
      The original footer was just one line of plain text: the business name, address, and phone — no map, no hours, no contact form link.

    Before Vs. After

    Design Decisions Made:

    • Mobile-first Layout
      Rebuilt the hero as a full-bleed image with text overlay - no floating columns that collapse on small screens.
    • Service card system
      Alternating image/text layout on desktop; clean stacked cards on mobile — each with a description and action button.
    • CTAs at every section
      "Explore Facials", "Explore Massages", "Find Packages" — each service section has a clear next step so visitors never have to hunt.
    • eGift Certificate section
      Gave the gift certificate its own dedicated section with a floral visual treatment, making it a visible revenue path rather than a buried nav item.
    • Functional footer
      Contact form link, phone, hours, address, and embedded map — all the info a client needs to book, in one place at the bottom of every page.

    Key Takeaway

    Most people searching for a local spa or massage therapist are on their phone. A site that breaks on mobile isn't just an inconvenience — it's a direct loss of clients. This redesign didn't change the brand; it made the existing brand actually work for the people most likely to book.

    Understanding the Problem

    The Research Phase

    I reviewed the existing site as a first-time visitor on both desktop and mobile, tracing the full journey from landing on the homepage to finding a service and taking action.

    What the Research Revealed

    The site was functional. Navigation worked — a services page was accessible from the top nav, and mobile had a menu icon. The issues weren't about broken links or missing pages. They were about the homepage not doing enough to keep a visitor engaged, and the mobile layout not holding up under real conditions.

    The most visible problem on mobile was the hero — the image and text used a side-by-side layout with no responsive breakpoint, so on a narrow screen they overlapped. The headline was partially obscured by the photo, making the opening impression harder to read than it should have been.

    Beyond that, the homepage had very little substance. Below the hero there was a single paragraph of introductory text, a large empty section, and a minimal one-line footer. No services were listed, no treatment imagery, and no calls to action. A visitor who wanted to know what the business offered had to navigate to the services page themselves — an extra step that the homepage gave them little reason to take.

    Visually, the site felt dated. The layout and aesthetic didn't reflect the quality of the experience being sold, and for a wellness business where trust and atmosphere matter, that gap was significant.

    Key Insights

    • Mobile readability was suffering. The hero overlap meant the first thing a phone user saw was a headline they had to work to read.
    • The homepage wasn't earning the visit. A visitor arriving for the first time had no immediate sense of what the business offered or why they should stay.
    • The visual presentation was underselling the brand. A premium, personal wellness experience deserves a site that feels that way from the first scroll.

    Pain Points That Drove Everything

    • Hero collapse on mobile — the opening impression was broken on the devices most likely to be used.
    • Empty homepage — there was nothing below the hero to build interest or guide the visitor forward.
    • Outdated visual design — the layout and spacing felt like an early-2000s site, not a modern wellness brand.
    • No calls to action — every next step required the visitor to figure it out themselves.
    • Footer didn't support conversion — no map, hours, or contact link at the point a visitor was ready to act.

    Hypothesis Going In

    • A full-bleed hero with text overlay would fix the mobile collapse and improve the first impression on both screen sizes.
    • Adding service cards to the homepage would let visitors understand the offering without navigating away.
    • A CTA after each service would remove the friction of figuring out what to do next.
    • A proper footer with phone, hours, address, map, and a contact link would convert visitors who were already ready to book.
    • A more modern visual treatment — better spacing, imagery, and layout — would bring the site in line with the quality of the business.

    Defining User Needs and Problems

    To redesign effectively, I needed to think about who was actually using this site and what they came to do — not just what looked broken from a design perspective.

    Who the user is

    The primary user is someone local to the Niles, Ohio area looking for a massage, facial, or wellness treatment. They're likely on their phone, probably found the business through a Google search or word of mouth, and are visiting the site to answer a few basic questions before deciding whether to book.

    They are not browsing casually. They have a specific intent — they want a service, they want to know what's available and what it costs, and they want to know how to book. The site has a matter of seconds to give them confidence that this is the right place.

    What the user needs

    • To immediately understand what the business offers. A visitor shouldn't have to click into a separate page just to find out what services exist.
    • To feel confident in the quality of the experience. Wellness is a trust-based purchase. The site itself needs to feel as calm and professional as the service being sold.
    • To find practical information without hunting. Phone number, location, hours, and how to book should be easy to find — especially on mobile.
    • A clear next step at every point. Whether they're ready to book immediately or still deciding, there should always be somewhere obvious to go.

    What the site was actually giving them

    The site wasn't broken in terms of navigation — a services page existed and was accessible from the top nav, and mobile had a menu icon. The real problem was first impressions and homepage substance.

    On mobile, the hero image and text overlapped, making the headline difficult to read. More broadly, the homepage gave visitors very little to work with — a single paragraph of introductory text, a large empty section, and a minimal footer. There were no services listed, no treatment images, and no calls to action on the page itself. To learn anything about what the business offered, a visitor had to take the extra step of navigating to the services page — something many wouldn't bother doing if the homepage hadn't already earned their interest.

    The site also felt visually dated. The layout, spacing, and overall aesthetic didn't reflect the quality of the experience being sold, which risked undermining trust before a visitor had read a single word about the treatments.

    The core problem statement

    The site was functional but the homepage wasn't doing enough work — a visitor on mobile struggled to read the hero, saw almost no content, and had little reason to stay or explore further before the site had established any credibility.

    The goal of the redesign wasn't to fix broken functionality — it was to bring the homepage up to the standard of the business it represented: more content, better responsiveness, and a layout that felt modern and professional from the first scroll.

    Ideate

    Turning problems into design decisions

    With the problems clearly defined, ideation was about working through each one and deciding how to solve it. Every decision made here had a direct reason tied back to the research.

    The hero

    The original hero placed the photo and text side by side — a layout that works at full desktop width but falls apart on smaller screens. The straightforward fix would have been to simply stack them vertically on mobile. But that approach would have made the hero feel small and generic.

    Instead I moved to a full-bleed image with the text overlaid directly on top. This solved the overlap issue entirely while also making the hero feel more immersive and modern — the kind of opening you'd expect from a professional wellness brand.

    Restructuring the navigation

    The original navigation had five items: Home, Services, Intake Form, eGift Certificate, and About. Clicking About revealed a dropdown with two options — the About page and the Contact Form. The Intake Form sat as its own separate top-level item. The Contact Form was hidden in a place visitors wouldn't think to look for it.

    The decision was to consolidate and clarify. The Intake Form was removed as a standalone nav item and grouped with the Contact Form under a new top-level item called Forms, which drops down to show both. About was simplified to only link to the About page — no more hidden contact option buried inside it. The result is a navigation that's easier to scan, more logically organized, and puts related items where visitors would actually expect to find them.

    Bringing services to the homepage

    The original site required visitors to navigate to a separate services page to understand what was on offer. The homepage gave them no preview, no imagery, and no reason to go looking.

    The decision here was to surface all five services — Facials, Massages, Body Treatments, Energy Work, and Packages — directly on the homepage as a series of cards. Each card includes a photo, a short description, and an explore button. Visitors get an immediate overview of the full offering without leaving the page, and those interested in a specific service have a clear path to find out more.

    Adding calls to action

    The original homepage had no prompts — no buttons, no links, nothing guiding a visitor toward the next step. This put the entire burden on the user to decide what to do.
    The redesign adds a clear CTA at every natural pause point: after each service card, on the eGift Certificate section, and in the footer. The intent is that at any point in the page a visitor could decide they're interested and immediately have somewhere to go — whether that's exploring a specific service, getting in touch, or purchasing a gift certificate.

    The eGift Certificate section

    Previously the eGift Certificate was only accessible through the navigation — invisible to anyone who didn't already know it existed. Gift certificates are a strong revenue stream for wellness businesses, particularly for people who want to give an experience as a gift but aren't sure which specific service to choose. The decision was to give it a dedicated section on the homepage with its own visual treatment. Surfacing it this way means it gets seen by every visitor rather than only those actively looking for it.

    Redesigning the footer

    The original footer was a single line of plain text. For a local business that operates by appointment, the footer is one of the most important parts of the page — it's where a convinced visitor goes to take action. The redesigned footer includes the logo, a link to the contact form, phone number, hours, address, and an embedded map. Everything a visitor needs to go from interested to booked is in one place at the bottom of every page.

    Visual direction

    Beyond the structural changes, the overall visual design needed to feel more modern and intentional. The original site used a flat olive-green background with script fonts throughout — a style that felt dated and didn't communicate the premium, calming atmosphere the business offers in person.

    The redesign keeps the brand's natural, earthy identity but applies it with more considered spacing, a cleaner layout, real photography for each service, and decorative touches — like the wildflower footer divider — that feel elegant rather than generic. The goal was a site that a visitor could look at and immediately feel like they were in good hands.

    Prototype

    Building the high-fidelity prototype

    With the design decisions made, I moved directly into building a high-fidelity prototype in Figma — skipping low-fidelity wireframes since I was working solo and the design itself was the primary deliverable.

    What was prototyped

    The scope of this project was the homepage only — the existing site's other pages (Services, About, Intake Form, eGift Certificate) were left intact. Because of this, the prototype is a high-fidelity static representation rather than a fully interactive clickable prototype. Buttons and links that would navigate to other pages are visually present but not wired up, since those destination pages were outside the scope of the redesign.
    The prototype was built across two frames — desktop and mobile — to show how the layout responds at both screen sizes. This was the core concern of the redesign, so demonstrating the behaviour at both breakpoints was the priority.

    What the prototype shows

    • Full-bleed hero with text overlay on both desktop and mobile — demonstrating that the layout holds at narrow widths without the overlap present in the original.
    • Service cards for each of the five offerings, with descriptions and explore buttons.
    • A dedicated eGift Certificate section with its own visual treatment.
    • CTAs at every section to guide visitors toward the next step.
    • A functional footer with contact info, hours, address, and an embedded map.
    • The mobile layout throughout — each section stacking cleanly and remaining readable on a narrow viewport.

    Limitations and scope

    Because only the homepage was redesigned, the prototype doesn't function as an end-to-end user journey. All buttons and links are visually present but not functional, as the destination pages were outside the scope of the redesign.
    In a fuller project — one where all pages were being redesigned — the next step would be wiring those connections up and running usability tests to observe how real users move through the site. For the scope of this project, the prototype serves its purpose: demonstrating that the homepage layout, hierarchy, and responsiveness now match what the business and its visitors actually need.

    Before and after — mobile hero

    The most significant improvement is on mobile. The original hero used a side-by-side float layout with no responsive breakpoint, causing the image and text to overlap on narrow screens. The redesign moves to a full-bleed image with text overlaid on top — fully readable at any screen size.

    Test & Learn

    Getting feedback from the client

    Once the high-fidelity prototype was complete I shared it directly with the owner of Vibrational Earth for review. This was the first real-world gut check on the design — did it work for the person who knows the business best?

    The feedback

    The owner responded positively to the redesign overall — the layout, the service cards, the hero, and the general direction all landed well. The one piece of actionable feedback was that she wanted to remove the eGift Certificate section entirely, as she had decided to stop offering them. This was a straightforward change to make — the section was removed from the design and the homepage updated accordingly. Everything else was approved as designed.

    What this showed

    Getting the business owner involved at the prototype stage — rather than after implementation — is exactly why this step matters. The eGift Certificate section had been a deliberate design decision based on what the site offered at the time of the audit. Had the design gone straight to build without a review, that section would have needed to be pulled out later at greater cost and effort.

    It's also a good reminder that designers work with information that has a shelf life. The audit captures the business as it is at a point in time — but businesses change, and getting sign-off from the client bridges that gap before anything is finalized.

    Final Design & Reflections

    From prototype to final design

    Two changes were made between the prototype and the final design — both came directly from feedback and honest review of the work. Neither was a major structural change, but both meaningfully improved the finished result.

    Removing the eGift Certificate section

    The prototype included a dedicated eGift Certificate section on the homepage — a deliberate design decision made during ideation to surface it as a visible revenue stream rather than a buried nav item. After sharing the prototype with the business owner, she let me know she had decided to stop offering gift certificates altogether.
    The section was removed cleanly. This is a straightforward example of why client review at the prototype stage matters — catching a business change before build rather than after saves time and avoids presenting visitors with something that no longer exists.

    Adding images to the mobile service cards

    The prototype kept the mobile service cards as text only — title, description, and a button. The thinking was that a lighter layout would be easier to scroll on a small screen. On reflection that didn't hold up. Without images the cards felt flat and gave no visual sense of what each service actually involved. For a wellness business where atmosphere is part of what's being sold, that was a real gap.

    A photo was added to the top of each mobile service card to match the desktop treatment. The change makes each service immediately distinguishable and gives the mobile layout the same visual weight as the desktop version.

    The final design

    With both changes applied, the design was complete. The homepage below represents the finished state — a full-bleed hero, a restructured navigation, five service cards with imagery and CTAs on both desktop and mobile, and a functional footer with contact details and an embedded map.

    Reflections

    This project started with a simple observation — a local business had a website that wasn't doing it justice. What it turned into was a full design process: auditing, defining problems, making deliberate decisions, getting feedback, and iterating. Here's what I took away from it.

    What Went Well

    Grounding every decision in the audit made the redesign feel purposeful rather than purely aesthetic. When the owner reviewed the prototype and approved almost everything, it confirmed that the problems identified at the start were the right ones to solve — and that solving them clearly was more valuable than chasing a visual style. The navigation restructure was one of the decisions I'm most satisfied with. It wasn't the most visible change but it was one of the most logical — consolidating related items, removing clutter from the top level, and making the menu easier to scan. Small structural decisions like that often have a bigger impact on usability than anything visual. Sharing the prototype with the client before finalizing anything also proved its worth. The eGift Certificate section was a considered design decision that became irrelevant because of a business change I had no way of knowing about. Catching that at the prototype stage rather than after the fact is exactly why that step exists.

    What I'd Do Differently

    I would spend more time with the business owner at the start — not just reviewing the site but understanding the business. What questions do clients ask most before booking? What do first-time visitors need to feel confident? What does she wish people knew before they arrived? That kind of insight shapes content decisions in ways that an audit of the existing site alone can't. If the scope allowed, I would also extend the redesign beyond the homepage. Right now there's a visual gap between the redesigned homepage and the existing pages a visitor reaches after clicking through — the experience isn't consistent end to end, which is a limitation worth acknowledging.

    Final Thoughts

    A redesign requires just as much process as starting from scratch — arguably more, because you're making a case for change rather than filling a blank canvas. The audit, the problem definition, the ideation — none of that was wasted work. It's what made the final design defensible rather than just different.

    It also reinforced how much the homepage carries for a small local business. For most visitors it's the only page they spend real time on before deciding whether to book or leave. A homepage that's responsive, clear, and representative of the business isn't a nice-to-have — it's the whole first impression.

Because of this, the prototype is a high-fidelity static representation rather than a fully interactive clickable prototype. Buttons and links that would navigate to other pages are visually present but not wired up, since those destination pages were outside the scope of the redesign.
    The prototype was built across two frames — desktop and mobile — to show how the layout responds at both screen sizes. This was the core concern of the redesign, so demonstrating the behaviour at both breakpoints was the priority.

    What the prototype shows

    • Full-bleed hero with text overlay on both desktop and mobile — demonstrating that the layout holds at narrow widths without the overlap present in the original.
    • Service cards for each of the five offerings, with descriptions and explore buttons.
    • A dedicated eGift Certificate section with its own visual treatment.
    • CTAs at every section to guide visitors toward the next step.
    • A functional footer with contact info, hours, address, and an embedded map.
    • The mobile layout throughout — each section stacking cleanly and remaining readable on a narrow viewport.

    Limitations and scope

    Because only the homepage was redesigned, the prototype doesn't function as an end-to-end user journey. All buttons and links are visually present but not functional, as the destination pages were outside the scope of the redesign.
    In a fuller project — one where all pages were being redesigned — the next step would be wiring those connections up and running usability tests to observe how real users move through the site. For the scope of this project, the prototype serves its purpose: demonstrating that the homepage layout, hierarchy, and responsiveness now match what the business and its visitors actually need.

    Before and after — mobile hero

    The most significant improvement is on mobile. The original hero used a side-by-side float layout with no responsive breakpoint, causing the image and text to overlap on narrow screens. The redesign moves to a full-bleed image with text overlaid on top — fully readable at any screen size.

    Test & Learn

    Getting feedback from the client

    Once the high-fidelity prototype was complete I shared it directly with the owner of Vibrational Earth for review. This was the first real-world gut check on the design — did it work for the person who knows the business best?

    The feedback

    The owner responded positively to the redesign overall — the layout, the service cards, the hero, and the general direction all landed well. The one piece of actionable feedback was that she wanted to remove the eGift Certificate section entirely, as she had decided to stop offering them. This was a straightforward change to make — the section was removed from the design and the homepage updated accordingly. Everything else was approved as designed.

    What this showed

    Getting the business owner involved at the prototype stage — rather than after implementation — is exactly why this step matters. The eGift Certificate section had been a deliberate design decision based on what the site offered at the time of the audit. Had the design gone straight to build without a review, that section would have needed to be pulled out later at greater cost and effort.

    It's also a good reminder that designers work with information that has a shelf life. The audit captures the business as it is at a point in time — but businesses change, and getting sign-off from the client bridges that gap before anything is finalized.

    Final Design & Reflections

    From prototype to final design

    Two changes were made between the prototype and the final design — both came directly from feedback and honest review of the work. Neither was a major structural change, but both meaningfully improved the finished result.

    Removing the eGift Certificate section

    The prototype included a dedicated eGift Certificate section on the homepage — a deliberate design decision made during ideation to surface it as a visible revenue stream rather than a buried nav item. After sharing the prototype with the business owner, she let me know she had decided to stop offering gift certificates altogether.
    The section was removed cleanly. This is a straightforward example of why client review at the prototype stage matters — catching a business change before build rather than after saves time and avoids presenting visitors with something that no longer exists.

    Adding images to the mobile service cards

    The prototype kept the mobile service cards as text only — title, description, and a button. The thinking was that a lighter layout would be easier to scroll on a small screen. On reflection that didn't hold up. Without images the cards felt flat and gave no visual sense of what each service actually involved. For a wellness business where atmosphere is part of what's being sold, that was a real gap.

    A photo was added to the top of each mobile service card to match the desktop treatment. The change makes each service immediately distinguishable and gives the mobile layout the same visual weight as the desktop version.

    The final design

    With both changes applied, the design was complete. The homepage below represents the finished state — a full-bleed hero, a restructured navigation, five service cards with imagery and CTAs on both desktop and mobile, and a functional footer with contact details and an embedded map.

    Reflections

    This project started with a simple observation — a local business had a website that wasn't doing it justice. What it turned into was a full design process: auditing, defining problems, making deliberate decisions, getting feedback, and iterating. Here's what I took away from it.

    What Went Well

    Grounding every decision in the audit made the redesign feel purposeful rather than purely aesthetic. When the owner reviewed the prototype and approved almost everything, it confirmed that the problems identified at the start were the right ones to solve — and that solving them clearly was more valuable than chasing a visual style. The navigation restructure was one of the decisions I'm most satisfied with. It wasn't the most visible change but it was one of the most logical — consolidating related items, removing clutter from the top level, and making the menu easier to scan. Small structural decisions like that often have a bigger impact on usability than anything visual. Sharing the prototype with the client before finalizing anything also proved its worth. The eGift Certificate section was a considered design decision that became irrelevant because of a business change I had no way of knowing about. Catching that at the prototype stage rather than after the fact is exactly why that step exists.

    What I'd Do Differently

    I would spend more time with the business owner at the start — not just reviewing the site but understanding the business. What questions do clients ask most before booking? What do first-time visitors need to feel confident? What does she wish people knew before they arrived? That kind of insight shapes content decisions in ways that an audit of the existing site alone can't. If the scope allowed, I would also extend the redesign beyond the homepage. Right now there's a visual gap between the redesigned homepage and the existing pages a visitor reaches after clicking through — the experience isn't consistent end to end, which is a limitation worth acknowledging.

    Final Thoughts

    A redesign requires just as much process as starting from scratch — arguably more, because you're making a case for change rather than filling a blank canvas. The audit, the problem definition, the ideation — none of that was wasted work. It's what made the final design defensible rather than just different.

    It also reinforced how much the homepage carries for a small local business. For most visitors it's the only page they spend real time on before deciding whether to book or leave. A homepage that's responsive, clear, and representative of the business isn't a nice-to-have — it's the whole first impression.
